Starvation and Aging in Operating Systems

Starvation and aging are key concepts in operating systems, primarily related to scheduling and resource allocation. These mechanisms are essential for understanding how operating systems handle resource contention and ensure fairness among processes.

Real Life Analogy: Imagine a queue in a busy grocery store:
  1. Starvation occurs when a shy customer (low-priority process) at the end of the line keeps letting others (higher-priority processes) go ahead, eventually never getting served.
  2. Aging is like a store policy that gradually moves the shy customer closer to the front of the queue the longer they wait.

Starvation

Starvation occurs when a process waits indefinitely for a resource due to the continuous preference given to higher-priority processes. It typically arises in scheduling algorithms like priority scheduling when low-priority processes are perpetually delayed.

Key Characteristics of Starvation:

  1. Low-priority processes remain unexecuted for a long time.
  2. Occurs due to unfair resource allocation policies.
  3. Common in scheduling systems that lack fairness mechanisms.

Causes of Starvation

  1. Strict priority scheduling that favors higher-priority processes.
  2. Excessive resource requests by high-priority processes.
  3. Insufficient mechanisms for fairness in resource allocation.

Solutions to Starvation

  1. Implement Aging: Gradually increase the priority of waiting processes to ensure fairness.
  2. Use Fair Scheduling Algorithms: Algorithms like Round Robin or Fair Share Scheduling prevent starvation by treating all processes equally.
  3. Limit Resource Holding Time: Restrict the amount of time a process can hold a resource.

Aging

Aging is a technique used to prevent starvation by gradually increasing the priority of a process as it waits in the queue. This ensures that all processes eventually get access to the CPU or other resources.

How Aging Works:

  1. Each process in the queue has a priority value assigned.
  2. The priority of waiting processes is incremented over time.
  3. Processes that have waited long enough gain sufficient priority to compete with newer processes.

Advantages of Aging

  1. Ensures fairness by preventing indefinite delays for low-priority processes.
  2. Improves overall system responsiveness and resource utilization.
  3. Compatible with various scheduling algorithms to enhance their fairness.

Disadvantages of Aging

  1. Increased complexity in scheduling algorithms.
  2. May lead to reduced efficiency for high-priority processes if not balanced properly.

Examples:
  • Disk Scheduling: In disk scheduling algorithms like SCAN or C-SCAN, starvation can occur when requests from one end of the disk are ignored for a long time. Aging can be applied to prevent this.
  • Process Scheduling: In priority-based process scheduling, aging ensures that low-priority processes eventually get executed.

Comparison Between Starvation and Aging

Starvation and aging are closely related concepts but serve opposite purposes in scheduling:
  1. Starvation: Describes a situation where a process waits indefinitely due to unfair prioritization.
  2. Aging: Is a solution that prevents starvation by dynamically increasing process priority.

In summary, aging is the remedy to starvation, ensuring that all processes are treated fairly over time.
